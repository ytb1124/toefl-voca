[00:04.52]#####_1_Man:_###### Let's talk a little bit about magical realism in literature. Magical realism is a genre that blends magical
elements with the real world, presenting fantastical events as normal aspects of everyday life. The critic Matthew Strecher defines it as
“what happens when a highly detailed, realistic setting is invaded by something too strange to believe.”

[00:27.34] Maybe you've heard about it in the context of Latin American authors like Gabriel Garcia Marquez and Isabel Allende, but we
also see it in works by authors outside Latin America like Salman Rushdie and Milan Kundera. The term actually comes from a German and
Italian painting style of the 1920s.

[00:47.89] In a magical realist work, characters might encounter ghosts, levitate, manipulate time—just as they might in a fantasy work—
but interestingly, these occurrences are never explained or questioned—they just coexist with reality.

[01:02.26] The result for readers is a new perspective on reality. Many authors have used this new perspective to address social and
political issues, so that the fantastical in a way comments on real-world problems. In Garcia Marquez's works, one purpose is to critique
things like colonialism and corruption.

[01:21.67] Of course, violating conventional narrative techniques requires a reader to have an open mind. So when you read a work like
Garcia Marquez's One Hundred Years of Solitude, you're challenged to rethink your perceptions and think about the deeper truths beneath
the surface of ordinary life.
